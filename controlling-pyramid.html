Sofia's Coffee Dream · Controlling

Five floors, three directions, one pyramid

Thomas Reichmann drew controlling as a building. Five levels of systems stacked on top of each other, the business functions running backwards into the depth, and time — operational against strategic — cutting through both. The uncomfortable part is where he put the front door: controlling only begins on the top two floors. Everything below is bookkeeping with a network cable.

Reichmann's controlling conception is system-supported: it does not start from what a controller should do, it starts from which systems hold which figures. Hover any floor, box or slice.

How to read the building

The floors are systems, the depth is functions, the diagonal is time

Read it bottom-up and it is an information supply chain. A sensor in the roasting drum records a bean temperature; the roast schedule decides which batch runs next; the cost accounting turns kilograms into euros; the analysis systems compare that against plan; the management information system condenses the lot into the handful of figures Sofia looks at before she opens the door.

Read it front-to-back and every floor exists once per function — sourcing, production, logistics, marketing, research and development. Read it along the diagonal and every floor splits into an operational and a strategic half. That is what makes the model multidimensional rather than merely layered.

Controlling starts on the fourth floor. In Reichmann's conception the lower three levels are operative Systeme: they capture, dispose and account. They produce data as a by-product of running the business, and none of it is controlling. Controlling begins where figures are analysed against a plan and condensed for a decision — the analysis and reporting systems and the management information system above them. This is the sentence students most often get wrong: the roast log is not controlling, the cost accounting is not controlling. They are the floors controlling stands on. Note what follows for a BI project: the orange line is exactly the boundary between the data warehouse and the reporting layer.

Reichmann's three dimensions are not decoration. Every controlling figure is addressed by all three at once: which function it belongs to, what kind of quantity it is, and whether it looks at this quarter or at 2029.

Twenty cells, one business

Pick a cell — it is an address, not a number

The grid is one horizontal slice through the pyramid. Each cell says: this function, measured this way, on this horizon. The same real event shows up in several cells with different values, and that is the point rather than a flaw — a sack of green coffee is a payment in January, a material cost in March when it is roasted, an expense in the quarter it is sold, and 60 kg the whole time.

The strategic column is the one worth arguing about. Switch the horizon and look at what happens to the evidence: operational figures come out of a system — the till, the ledger, the roast log. Strategic figures mostly do not exist yet. Landed cost corridor 2027–2029 is not in any database; it is an assumption with a number attached. Reichmann's model puts both in the same building on purpose, because the strategic figure is only trustworthy if somebody can trace it back down to the floors where the operational one is measured. A strategy without that staircase is a wish.

Reichmann and Lachnit built the key figure system that fills the top floor. Its trick: two top figures of equal rank — profitability and liquidity — because a business can die of either.

The part that only exists for Sofia

The general part above compares Sofia with a hairdresser. The special part is company-specific: figures nobody else's chart of accounts contains, chosen because they are the ones that actually move the two top figures.

Reading the system

Two peaks, not one

Most key figure systems have a single apex — DuPont drives everything into return on investment. Reichmann and Lachnit refused that in 1976 and gave the system two, because a firm pursues several goals at once and a profitable quarter with an empty account is still a crisis. The system is split a second time, into a general part that is industry-neutral and a special part that is not.

Hover any line or tile for what it is and where it comes from.

Watch the roasting loss. It sits in the special part, and in the general part it is invisible — no line of the P&L is called roasting loss. Yet it is the single figure with the most leverage on the top figure: cutting it from 16 % to 14 % on roughly 6 000 kg of green coffee a year releases around 120 kg of sellable roasted coffee that Sofia has already paid for. That is why Reichmann split the system in two. A key figure system built only from the ledger will produce perfectly correct numbers and never once point at the thing worth fixing.

Reichmann is one of four answers German controlling research gave to the same question: what is controlling actually for? His answer is the narrowest — and the one that maps straight onto a BI architecture.

Four conceptions

The argument, in one line each

Reichmann says controlling supplies information. Horváth says it coordinates planning and control. Küpper widens that to coordinating the entire management system. Weber and Schäffer move it again, to securing the rationality of management. Each later position accuses the earlier one of being too narrow; each earlier one can accuse the later one of being too vague to build anything from.

The same building, drawn by a BI architect

Reichmann's five floors and the BI reference architecture describe the same road with different vocabulary. He names the systems; the architecture names the layers the data passes through.

Why this concept aged well. Reichmann's pyramid was drawn for mainframes and has since been redrawn as a House of Controlling, with an integrated management information system at the top and the analysis and reporting floor implemented as IT-supported reporting cubes. Change the labels and it is a modern BI stack: capture, operational systems, accounting, data warehouse and reporting, cockpit. What has not moved is the floor where controlling starts — which is exactly the line a BI project has to defend when someone asks for "a dashboard on the raw data". You can see the same road drawn the other way round in the BI reference architecture module.

The one sentence

Controlling does not start where the data starts. It starts two floors up. Sofia's till, her roasting drum and her ledger produce figures all day, and not one of them is controlling. Only where a figure is measured against a plan and condensed for a decision does the building become a controlling system — everything below is the staircase.
